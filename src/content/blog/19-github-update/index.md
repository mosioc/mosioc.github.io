In my last post I wrote that my account came back with no explanation. That was true at the time. Then the reply arrived, and I want to go through it carefully, because what it leaves out says more than what it includes.

Here is the message I received on September 30, 11:13 PM UTC:

> Our apologies for the delay in getting back to you; we are currently dealing with higher than usual request volumes and are working to respond to priority issues such as yours as quickly as possible.
>
> Sometimes our abuse detecting systems highlight accounts that need to be manually reviewed.
>
> We've cleared the restrictions from your account, so you have full access to GitHub again.

## I Had Already Read This Email

When I received it, I recognized it.

Over those 45 days, while searching for the reason my account was flagged, I kept landing on GitHub discussions and threads from other people in the same situation. And in those threads I kept seeing the same two messages: the automatic one you get right after submitting a ticket, and a final reply with the same wording as the one I got.

That changes how I read it. The line about abuse detection systems, the apology for the delay: this isn't a message written about my account. It's a template. It's what the system sends when a case is closed.

I'm not saying that to blame the person who sent it. Support teams use templates, and that's normal. But it explains the missing pieces. There is no explanation of what triggered the flag because the template has no place for it.

It also shows something about how people cope. Flagged developers don't get answers from the platform, so they go to forums and Reddit and compare notes with each other: how long did yours take, did you get this message, what did you try. Community support fills the space that official support leaves empty.

That is the whole explanation. Let me go through it.

## "Priority issues such as yours"

I waited 45 days. I sent a reinstatement request and four follow-ups, and I contacted people with connections inside GitHub.

I'll be fair: this is probably a standard line, not something written for my case. But it is still the sentence I received after 45 days, and I can't read it without noticing the distance between the wording and what I lived through.

## "Sometimes our abuse detecting systems highlight accounts"

This is the most important line, and it is also the most carefully worded.

It confirms what I suspected: an automated system flagged me. It says "highlight", not "ban" or "suspend". The system highlights, and a human is supposed to review. But in practice, between the highlight and the review, my account was locked and my work was unreachable for 45 days.

So the automated part is fast, and the human part is slow. The machine accuses in seconds and the person clears it in weeks. And all that time, the weight of proof sits on the accused.

## The Part That Stays With Me

On one hand, I understand it. A platform at that scale needs automated abuse detection, and abuse is real. False positives are probably unavoidable. I don't think the existence of the system is the problem.

The problem is what happens after a false positive. A system that is allowed to be wrong should be built to correct itself quickly, because the person on the other side is paying for its mistakes. Instead the cost lands entirely on the user: my time, my deadlines, my portfolio, my stress. And the platform's cost was one delayed email.

## Still, Thank You

I don't want this post to end on bitterness, because it isn't how I feel.

My account is back. My repos are there, my history is intact, and I can work again. I am very happy about that, and I'm grateful to GitHub for restoring it. The delay was long and the silence was hard, but in the end someone looked at my case, cleared it, and gave me my work back.

Both things are true at the same time: the process failed me for 45 days, and I'm thankful it was fixed. I think it's possible, and honest, to say both.

## Final Thought

The last line of the email says: *"Please let me know if you need anything else."*

I do have one request. I'd like the next person in my position to wait days, not weeks, and to know why. I don't expect an answer to that, but I wanted it written down in public, with the dates attached.

For now, my account is back, my work is safe, and I'm grateful. Back to building.
